A gallery in words

Most reproductions of his paintings are still under copyright somewhere — Qi Baishi died in 1957, and the standard life-plus-70 clock hasn’t run out yet in the US or most of Europe. Where we found a work with a clear public-domain or CC-licensed source, it’s here, credited. Where we couldn’t, you get a small ink sketch of our own instead of a borrowed jpeg. Visit the further reading page for museums that hold the real thing.

Recurring subjects, roughly by the decade they came to define him
Image Subject Decade What’s on the paper
Shrimp 1920s–50s His single most famous motif. Translucent bodies built from a handful of grey-black strokes, legs added almost as an afterthought, yet the whole animal reads as weightless and underwater. He kept live shrimp on his desk for years to study how the segments caught the light.
Crabs 1920s–40s Painted with the same economy as the shrimp but heavier, more argumentative — ink blots for the shell, spidery legs radiating outward, usually a small procession of two or three rather than one.
Chicks and roosters 1930s–40s Fluffy, faintly comic hatchlings rendered in soft wash, often paired with a single scratchy ink line for a worm or a grain of rice — the whole drama of the painting compressed into that one line.
Vegetables and gourds 1930s–50s Garlic bulbs, loofahs, radishes, ears of corn: kitchen produce elevated to the same seriousness as a scholar’s bamboo, painted in flat washes of colour rather than the monochrome ink favoured by more academic painters.
Insects throughout his career Grasshoppers, cicadas, dragonflies — painted with startling, almost scientific precision, often set against a loosely brushed flower or leaf so the contrast in detail does the work a caption would otherwise do.
Mice 1940s A late, wry addition — small ink-black rodents, usually caught mid-theft around a candle or a stack of fruit, closer to a cartoonist’s line than a court painter’s.
