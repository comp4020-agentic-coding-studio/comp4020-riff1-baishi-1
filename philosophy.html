Between likeness and unlikeness

Qi Baishi’s most quoted line about his own work says more about how to look at any picture than most art-school lectures manage in a semester:

“The excellence of painting lies in the fact that it is between likeness and unlikeness. Too much likeness flatters the vulgar taste; too little likeness deceives the world.”

It sounds like a modest hedge — not too real, not too fake — but it is really a rejection of both of the easy exits available to a painter. Paint something so faithfully that it could be a photograph, and you have made a technical exercise, not a picture with a point of view: pure likeness flatters an audience that mistakes accuracy for skill. Paint something so loosely that the subject dissolves into gesture, and you have made a private mark nobody else can read: pure unlikeness is a kind of dishonesty dressed up as freedom, because it stops being about the shrimp at all.

What this looks like on the paper

His shrimp are the clearest demonstration. Count the strokes on one and there are startlingly few — a handful of grey washes for the body, a scatter of dark dots for the joints, two flicked lines for the long antennae. No shrimp actually looks like that. And yet every one of his shrimp looks unmistakably, specifically alive: weightless, faintly translucent, caught mid-drift the way a real shrimp hangs in still water. The likeness is not in the outline. It is in the few details he refused to simplify — the exact curl of the tail, the precise pressure that makes one antenna heavier than the other — set against everything else he was willing to leave out.

That is the discipline behind “between”: knowing which two or three details are load-bearing, and having the nerve to let go of the rest. Get the load-bearing details wrong and no amount of surrounding detail saves the picture. Get them right and the picture survives having almost nothing else on it.

Why a coding studio shrine cares

It is a useful thing to sit with while directing an agent, too. A spec that is too literal — every pixel prescribed, every case enumerated — produces the software equivalent of pure likeness: technically compliant, and dead on arrival. A spec so loose it is really just a vibe produces the equivalent of pure unlikeness: nothing anyone can check against, including you. The useful brief, like the useful brushstroke, names the two or three things that actually carry the meaning and leaves everything else to be decided in the making.
