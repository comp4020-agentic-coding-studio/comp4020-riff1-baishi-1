The carpenter who became a master

Qi Baishi was born in 1864 to a farming family in Xiangtan, in China’s Hunan province — poor enough that formal schooling lasted barely a year before he was needed for farm work. What he had instead was an eye, and it found its first outlet in wood.

The woodcarver’s apprenticeship

In his teens he apprenticed as a carpenter and woodcarver, carving the kind of decorative furniture panels that Hunan households wanted: flowers, birds, auspicious symbols. It was steady, skilled work, and it taught him to draw before anyone had called him a painter. A chance encounter with a set of painting manuals in a patron’s house — the kind of block-printed instruction books that circulated among local artisans — gave him his first real access to a painting tradition he had no formal way into otherwise.

Provincial fame, then a house in Beijing

Through his twenties and thirties he built a reputation across Hunan as a portraitist and decorative painter, studying informally under local scholar-painters and slowly assembling the technical range — landscape, figure, calligraphy, seal-carving — expected of a serious literati painter, despite having none of the literati’s formal education. In his fifties, with the countryside unsettled by banditry and warlord conflict, he left Hunan for Beijing and started again, in his own late-life words, largely broke.

Beijing did not receive him warmly at first — his early style was considered coarse next to the refined, deliberately faded literati painting fashionable in the capital. The turning point, by his own account and that of his biographers, was encouragement from the painter Chen Shizeng to stop imitating that fashion and paint in his own hand instead. He did, and it worked: bolder colour, plainer compositions, and everyday subjects — shrimp, crabs, chicks, garlic, loofahs — painted with a few decisive strokes rather than the accumulated, tonal layering the capital expected.

A national figure, painting until the end

By his final decades Qi Baishi was one of the most celebrated painters in China, eventually holding honorary positions at the Central Academy of Fine Arts and the Chinese Artists Association, and receiving the World Peace Council’s International Peace Prize in 1955 in recognition of his work. He kept painting into his nineties, prolifically and without much ceremony about it — by some estimates leaving behind tens of thousands of works. He died in Beijing in 1957, having spent roughly the last third of his life as one of the most reproduced painters the country had ever had, and the first third of it carving flowers into other people’s furniture.
